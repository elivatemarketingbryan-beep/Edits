Research date: 2026-10-07. User-provided reference: [@jackroberts___ reels](https://www.instagram.com/jackroberts___/reels/?hl=en).

This is the initial four-reel record. A later [variety review](VARIETY_REVIEW.md) revisits three samples and adds a fifth reel, with more detailed observations of medium, material, typography, and layout.

## Scope and evidence quality

These notes come from direct inspection of the public reel grid and visible video playback in Chrome, using the existing Instagram session. They are an initial **four-reel visual sample**, not a completed corpus analysis. Three recent reels and one older pinned reel were inspected. Several playback frames were observed for the recent samples; no reel was reviewed continuously with audible sound. Player time, duration, and source dimensions were read from the displayed video element. Times below are approximate screenshot observation points, not edit boundaries.

This research is based on the direct observations below. No creator videos, audio, screenshots, transcripts, characters, logos, or designs are bundled with these notes. The durable deliverable is the source-linked observation record.

The text-only web fetch of Instagram was unavailable to the main research process, but Chrome playback was accessible. A supported temporary download attempt encountered an unsupported blob source; it did not save media. No bulk collection was performed.

## Patterns supported by this sample

- Recent split scenes are approximately 50:50 vertically, with the explaining visual above and the presenter below. The presenter is framed tightly enough to read facial expression.
- Full-height visual takeovers are part of the grammar. The speaker does not need to remain visible at every moment.
- Sampled speech captions are brief, commonly one to three words. They use heavy white sans-serif lettering, dark edging/shadow, and a translucent dark rounded background. Their position follows the layout: at the seam during split scenes, below the main artwork during full-height scenes.
- Supporting visuals are tied to meaning: inspect writing with a magnifier, flag errors with flags, show brand colors as swatches, contrast animation quality with a pixelated display, represent a portfolio with a gallery.
- Palettes and graphic materials vary with the idea. The repeated structure is a stable presenter/caption relationship plus purposeful supporting imagery, not one compulsory visual skin.
- Visual hierarchy favors one large object or small group of related objects. Secondary cards, accents, or examples sit behind or around that focus.

These are observations of the sample. Exact font families, animation libraries, transition curves, keyframe timings, soundtrack treatment, sound-effect frequency, source-asset provenance, complete spoken transcripts, and average shot length were **not established**. Similar-looking animation does not establish which software produced it.

## Implementation hypotheses to test, not creator facts

1. Choose a visual construction from the meaning of each beat: comparison, transformation, sequence, inspection, hierarchy, measurement, or concrete demonstration. Author new content-specific graphics in code.
2. Use split-screen when facial expression and explanation both add value. Use a full-height takeover when the supporting visual needs room to be understood. Restore the presenter when it helps establish a new point or connection.
3. Keep caption placement linked to composition so it does not cover the essential evidence or the speaker's mouth.
4. Derive color and material choices from the video's subject, audience, and available source assets. Preserve readable contrast and a coherent treatment within a given sequence.
5. Build a representative corpus with explicit annotations before claiming reliable style matching across topics. The present four-reel sample supports a first grammar, not proof that arbitrary raw footage can be edited successfully without review.

Useful future annotation fields: reel URL, publication date, topic, source duration, observation method, reviewed time ranges, layout intervals, caption position, main visual purpose, visual medium, palette family, transition observations, and confidence. Keep unknown fields unknown. Use user-authorized local reference media for frame-by-frame or audio analysis if a fuller corpus is added later; package annotations and original project assets for members.

## Tooling facts checked against primary sources

These facts are separate from the visual research. They do not establish what Jack used.

- **HyperFrames** here means [HeyGen's HyperFrames repository](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes), which describes deterministic video rendering from HTML, CSS, media, and seekable animation. The repository lists Apache-2.0 and Node.js 22+/FFmpeg requirements. Its [quickstart](https://hyperframes.heygen.com/quickstart) says local rendering does not consume HeyGen credits; optional hosted/media services can have costs.
- The official [HyperFrames/Remotion comparison](https://hyperframes.heygen.com/guides/hyperframes-vs-remotion) describes HyperFrames' seekable HTML timeline and Remotion's frame-driven React components. Both are code-rendering options; neither requires generated video footage.
- The [HyperFrames talking-head guide](https://hyperframes.heygen.com/guides/captions-and-recuts) distinguishes caption/overlay workflows, which preserve the existing spoken cut, from changing the footage/edit itself. Raw-footage editing therefore needs a distinct editorial selection and assembly stage.
- [Remotion pricing](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/license/pricing) lists free use for individuals and organizations up to three people, with a company license for qualifying larger teams/collaborations. The automation tier is currently $0.01 per render with a $100 monthly minimum. The [license FAQ](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/license/faq) explains collaboration/headcount rules and source-project ownership. Member distribution should retain a link to current terms rather than promise that every member's use is free. Remotion is source-available under its own license.

Tool versions and licensing can change. Verify the chosen dependency's current requirements when packaging a release.
